# Coinflip Mind, Video 2: Why $9.99 Works on Your Brain

**Title:** Why $9.99 Works on Your Brain
**Hook (scored):** "It's only nine bucks." You've said it. It was $10. Why does your brain keep losing this?
(/ig-reel 65, /yt-script 58: best combined of 11 tested; also the most relatable)
**Fix named:** The Round-Up Rule

---

## Cold open

"It's only nine bucks."

You've said it. Maybe this week.

It was ten dollars.

And here's the weird part. You knew. You've known since you were a kid that $9.99 is basically ten. Everybody knows.

It still works on you. It works on me. It works on people who price cars for a living. It even shows up in hospitals. We'll get to both.

So why does your brain keep losing this? And what is it actually costing you?

Because it's not one cent. It's not even close.

## The first number wins

Try something with me. I'm going to show you a price. Don't think. Just notice the first thing your brain says.

Ready?

$4.99.

If your brain said "four-something," you just felt it happen.

Here's what's going on. You read left to right. And your brain is impatient. It grabs the first digit, decides how big the number is, and moves on. The rest of the price shows up a split second later. Too late. The decision's already made.

So $4.99 gets filed under "four." $5.00 gets filed under "five." One cent apart on the tag. A whole dollar apart in your head.

Researchers call this the left-digit effect.

In 2005, two marketing professors, Manoj Thomas and Vicki Morwitz, tested it in five experiments. When one cent changed the first digit, like $3.00 down to $2.99, people saw a real drop in price. When one cent changed a digit further right, they barely noticed.

Same one cent. The only thing that mattered was the first number.

## "But I'm not dumb"

Now you might be thinking: okay, but I'm not dumb. I know $4.99 is five dollars.

And you're right. If you stop and think, you know.

But be honest. When do you actually stop and think?

You're in a store with hundreds of prices. You're scrolling at 1 a.m. You're tired, you're hungry, your thumb is already on "Add to cart." Your brain isn't doing math right now. It's taking shortcuts. And the shortcut is: read the first number, move on.

Here's the sneaky part. Later, when you try to remember what you paid, you don't remember the price. You remember the shortcut. "It was like nine bucks."

Stores don't need you to be bad at math. They just need you to be busy.

## The dress that sold more at a higher price

So how much can one little 9 actually do? Two researchers, Eric Anderson and Duncan Simester, found out with a women's clothing catalog.

They printed different versions of the same catalog and mailed them to random customers. Same dress. Same photo. Only the price changed.

Some people saw it for $34. Some saw $39. Some saw $44.

Quick, which one sold the most?

Normal logic says $34. It's the cheapest.

Nope. At $39, the dress sold about a third more than at $34.

And at $44? About the same as $34.

They raised the price by five dollars, and sold more.

Why? Because a 9 at the end doesn't just say "cheap." It says "deal." You've seen 99 on a million sale signs. So now your brain reads it as "good price." Even when nobody checked.

And people really like that feeling. One company learned that the hard way.

## The store that took the deals away

In 2012, JCPenney decided to stop playing games with prices. No more constant sales. No more coupons. Just simple, everyday prices. They called it "fair and square."

Sounds great, right?

Customers hated it. That year, sales at its established stores fell about 25 percent. The company lost almost a billion dollars. And the CEO admitted the pricing plan was a mistake.

Shoppers didn't just want low prices. They wanted the feeling of getting a deal.

So think about the last thing you bought because it felt like a deal. Did you check the price? Or did the feeling check it for you?

## Why some prices never end in 9

But wait. If 9s work so well, why does a nice restaurant charge $28 and not $27.99? Why do fancy brands love round numbers?

Because round numbers sell a different feeling.

In 2015, researchers Monica Wadhwa and Kuangjie Zhang tested this. When people were buying a camera for a family vacation, it did better at a round $100. When the same camera was for a class project, it did better at $98.76.

Round numbers feel right when you're buying with your heart. Exact numbers feel right when you're buying with your head.

So a 9 says "smart deal." A round number says "treat yourself."

Either way, the price was picked to match the feeling. Not the other way around.

## Even car dealers fall for it

Okay, but maybe that's just regular shoppers. Professionals don't fall for this, right?

This is my favorite study. Economists looked at more than 22 million used cars sold at wholesale auctions. The buyers there are car dealers. People whose whole job is knowing what a car is worth.

And they found something ridiculous.

Every time the odometer crosses a 10,000-mile mark, the price drops. Take a car at 79,999 miles, and the same kind of car at 80,000. Basically the same car. They can sell a couple hundred dollars apart.

One extra mile. About two hundred dollars.

Because 79,999 reads as "seventy-something." 80,000 reads as "eighty."

If car dealers can't turn this off, with real money on the line, then you're not the problem. This is just how human brains read numbers.

## It's not just money

And it's not only prices. It's any number.

In 2020, researchers looked at about 70,000 heart attack patients in the US. They compared people who came in just before their 80th birthday with people who came in just after.

Two weeks apart in age. Basically the same patients.

Just before 80, about 7 percent got bypass surgery. Just after 80, about 5 percent.

At 79 and 81, there was no jump like that. Only at the moment the first digit flipped from 7 to 8.

The researchers say they can't completely rule out other reasons. But the best explanation is the same one. 79 reads as "seventies." 80 reads as "eighty."

If the first digit can shape a decision about heart surgery, it can definitely shape what's in your cart.

## Where it's costing you

So where is it hitting you? Let's look.

Start with subscriptions. Say you've got four of them at $14.99. In your head, each one is "fourteen bucks." The real total is about $60 a month. That's $720 a year.

Then there's what I call the "under" rule. You tell yourself, "I'll get one under fifty." And then you buy the one for $49.99. Your rule worked perfectly. For the store.

And it gets bigger as the price gets bigger. A laptop at $999 feels like "nine hundred something." It's a thousand dollars. A car at $29,995 feels like "twenty-something thousand." It's thirty.

But here's what it really costs you. Not the pennies.

It costs you the pause. That one second where you would've thought, "Wait, that's ten dollars. Do I even want this for ten?"

That pause is where your money gets saved. And the 9 is designed to skip it.

## The Round-Up Rule

So how do you beat a trick that works on car dealers? You don't try harder. You give your brain a new habit.

Call it the Round-Up Rule.

One: say the real number. Not "nine ninety-nine." Say "ten dollars." Not "forty-nine ninety-nine." Say "fifty." It feels silly, but you're making your brain file the real number instead of the shortcut.

Two: ask the round question. Before you buy, ask yourself: "Would I pay a flat ten for this? A flat fifty?" If your answer changes, the 9 was doing the selling.

Three: times twelve. For anything monthly, multiply by twelve before you hit subscribe. $14.99 a month is about $180 a year. Is it worth $180? Sometimes yes. But now you're actually deciding.

## Outro

Stores aren't counting on you being bad at math. They're counting on you not stopping to do it.

So the next time you hear yourself say "it's only nine bucks"...

Round up.

It's ten. And now you'll notice.
