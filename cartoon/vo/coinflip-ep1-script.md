## Chapter 1: Intro

You spend $40 in seconds, but never save it. Why does your brain stop you?

Think about it. Food delivery, $40. Tap, tap, done. You didn't even feel it.

But moving that same $40 into savings? Suddenly it feels like you're losing something.

Most people say that's just discipline. Some people have it, some don't.

But that's not what's going on.

The real reason is weirder. Your brain thinks future you is a stranger.

In this video: the brain scan that proved it. A five-second test you can take on yourself. The trick that almost
quadrupled people's savings. And one rule you can set up today, so saving happens without you even trying.

## Chapter 2: Meet Sam

Meet Sam. Sam is 25, has a job, and every month, Sam promises: this month, I'll save.

And every month, the same thing happens. A dinner. A pair of shoes. A sale that ends tonight. By the 30th, there's
nothing left to save.

Sam isn't bad with money. Sam is normal. And to understand why, we need to look inside Sam's head.

## Chapter 3: The Stranger Inside Your Head

In 2009, researchers at Stanford put people in a brain scanner. Then they asked them to think about four people.
Themselves today. Themselves in ten years. A celebrity today. And that same celebrity in ten years.

When people thought about themselves today, one part of their brain lit up. When they thought about the celebrity, it
was quieter.

And when they thought about themselves ten years from now? Their brain looked more like it was thinking about the
celebrity than about themselves.

To your brain, future you is almost someone else.

Here's the part that matters for your money. The people whose future self looked most like a stranger were also the
least patient. Offered a smaller amount now or a bigger amount later, they were more likely to take the money now.

So when Sam skips saving, Sam isn't lazy. Sam's brain is making a very normal choice: me, right now, or a stranger I'll
never meet. And it picks right now. Every time.

So how far away is future you, for you? There's a way to check. It takes five seconds.

## Chapter 4: The Two-Circle Test

Picture two circles. One is you today. The other is you in ten years.

Now pick the picture that feels right. Are the circles far apart, barely touching? Overlapping a little? Or almost on
top of each other?

Pause and pick one. Seriously.

Researchers used a test like this on 153 Americans. The people who chose circles that overlapped more, the people who
felt connected to their future self, had built up more savings.

So if your circles were far apart, now you know why saving feels so hard. It's not a character flaw. It's distance.

And distance can be shrunk. But first, you need to see who's on the other side of the fight.

## Chapter 5: Why Spending Feels So Easy

Why does spending feel so easy?

Because the reward is instant. Tap the button, the food's on its way, and your brain gets its hit of anticipation right
now.

Saving is the opposite. You give something up today, and the reward goes to the stranger. In twenty years.

That's not a fair fight. Right-now you wins almost every time.

And look at how spending is designed. One tap to buy. Your card already saved. "Arrives tomorrow." "Pay later in four easy
payments."

Every one of those removes a moment where you might stop and think. And "pay later" is the cleverest: it hands the
cost to the same stranger you're not saving for.

Sam's phone is built to talk to right-now Sam. Nothing on it talks to future Sam. So what is future Sam actually losing?

## Chapter 6: What The Stranger Loses

One $40 delivery a week is $2,080 a year. Easy to spend, because each one is small.

Now picture $100 a month going into savings instead, starting at 25. Say it grows 7% a year. That's an assumption,
not a promise. By 65, Sam would have put in $48,000, and future Sam would have about $262,000.

Start at 35 instead. Same $100 a month. Future Sam gets about $122,000.

Waiting ten years just cost future Sam around $140,000.

That's the stranger's side of the deal. Right-now Sam never sees that number. So it never counts.

Which raises the real question: can you make your brain see it? Researchers tried something that sounds like science fiction.

## Chapter 7: Meet Future You

In 2011, researchers tried to make the stranger less of a stranger. They took photos of young adults and digitally aged them, so each person could
look at a realistic version of themselves decades older.

Then they asked everyone how they'd split money between spending now and saving for retirement.

The people who had just looked their older self in the eyes chose to set aside more.

It was a lab experiment, and the money was hypothetical, so don't treat it like magic. But the idea is powerful: when
future you has a face, your brain starts to care about them.

But the biggest result didn't use a single photo.

## Chapter 8: The Trick That Almost Quadrupled Savings

Two economists, Richard Thaler and Shlomo Benartzi, found something even stronger.

They noticed that people hate feeling a loss today. Taking money out of this paycheck hurts.

So they designed a plan called Save More Tomorrow. Instead of asking people to save more now, they asked them to agree
to save more later: a slice of every future raise.

Saying yes cost nothing today. Their paycheck never shrank. Savings only grew when a raise arrived, so it never felt
like losing money.

At the first company that tried it, 78% of the people offered the plan joined. Their average savings rate went from
3.5%… to 13.6%, in 40 months.

Same people. Same brains. They just stopped asking willpower to do the work.

## Chapter 9: "But I Don't Earn Enough"

Maybe you're thinking: that's nice, but I don't earn enough to save.

Fair. So start smaller than feels useful. $25 a paycheck. If you're paid every two weeks, that's $650 by the end of the
year, without ever feeling a big loss.

The amount matters less than the habit. Save More Tomorrow worked because people started small and let it grow with
their raises. Once the habit runs on its own, making it bigger is easy.

## Chapter 10: The Autopilot Rule

So here's the fix. Call it the Autopilot Rule: never make saving a decision you have to make again.

Every time saving is a choice, right-now you gets a vote. And right-now you wins.

So take the choice away.

One: set up an automatic transfer to savings on the day your paycheck lands, before you ever see the money. Even $25 a
paycheck counts.

Two: pre-commit your raises. Next time you get one, send part of it straight to savings, before you get used to it.

Three: give future you a face. Rename your savings account "Future Me." It sounds silly, but you just turned a
stranger into a person.

Four: make spending one step slower. Delete your saved card from the app you overspend in. That one extra step gives
future you a chance to speak up.

## Chapter 11: Outro

Your brain isn't broken. It's doing exactly what it was built to do: take care of the person it can see.

So let it see future you.

Because the person you're saving for isn't a stranger. It's you. Just a little later.
